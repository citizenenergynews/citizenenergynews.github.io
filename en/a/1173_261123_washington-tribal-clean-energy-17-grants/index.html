Washington State gives $20.74 million to 17 tribal solar projects

AI Reporter Kim · Written and source-checked by AIAI translation2026.11.23

Washington State is using revenue from carbon allowance sales to help tribes put solar and batteries on homes, a college and a fire station. It funded all 16 projects that missed out in 2025 for lack of money.

The Washington State Department of Commerce announced on November 20 (local time) that it will provide $20,742,913 to 17 tribal clean energy projects. The Legislature approved the money in the 2026 supplemental state budget, and it comes from revenue under the Climate Commitment Act, the state program that caps emissions and sells allowances. Eligible applicants are federally recognized tribes whose traditional lands include parts of Washington, and the tribes themselves decide what to install.

Seven of the 17 projects put solar on tribal housing and social service facilities. The Colville Confederated Tribes will use $487,594 to install solar and batteries at a housing development home to 27 low-income households. The Nez Perce Tribe will use $954,288 to spread 240kW of solar and 560kWh of batteries across tribal homes to lower utility bills. The Swinomish Indian Tribal Community received three awards, and the largest, $2,370,133, will put 228kW of solar and 228kW of battery backup on 30 tribal homes.

The awards also cover equipment that keeps public services running during outages. Northwest Indian College will use $2,469,342 to build a campus microgrid with 124kW of solar and 1,876kWh of batteries. The Swinomish fire department will get 15kW of solar and 72kW of batteries for $1,103,959. The Muckleshoot Housing Authority will use $378,994 to put 93.8kW of solar on a planned parking canopy.

Some projects are still at the planning stage. The Tulalip Tribes received $200,000 for design and permitting of a planned 5MW solar farm on a former landfill. The money fills the gap for 16 projects that were turned down in the 2025 round, when requests exceeded available funding by more than $20 million, and for one project that received only partial funding.

Citizen Energy View

Washington State has steered $20.74 million raised by selling carbon allowances into home solar and microgrids for tribal communities. The key point is that it revived all 16 projects rejected for lack of money with a supplemental budget the following year. If Korea spent its Climate Response Fund, which receives revenue from auctioned emission allowances, first on sunlight income villages and solar for energy-poor households, resident-owned power plants could ease worries about both bills and blackouts. The government and the National Assembly should start by creating a way to pick up villages rejected in one round with the next year's budget.
