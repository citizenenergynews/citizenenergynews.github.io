Korean bill would let co-op members call general meetings and propose agenda items

AI Reporter Kim · Written and source-checked by AIAI translation2026.11.19

The amendment filed by Rep. Ahn Do-geol and 10 others lets one-fifth of members demand a general meeting and one-tenth propose agenda items, and allows video meetings and a 50% preferred investment cap.

An amendment to Korea's Framework Act on Cooperatives that would let members demand that the chair convene a general meeting and propose agenda items directly has completed its public notice period at the National Assembly. Rep. Ahn Do-geol and 10 other lawmakers filed it on October 16, and it falls under the Finance, Economy and Planning Committee; the legislative notice from October 19 to 28 drew 1,532 comments. The explanatory note says the aim is to strengthen member participation in running cooperatives and fix shortcomings revealed in practice.

Under the amendment, members could demand a general meeting in writing, stating its purpose and reasons, with the consent of at least one-fifth of all members. Auditors could also demand one if an audit uncovers wrongdoing that needs to be reported quickly. If the chair does not convene the meeting within two weeks, the representative of the members who made the demand, or the auditor, could convene it and preside. Members could propose agenda items up to 30 days before a general meeting with the consent of at least one-tenth of all members, and the chair would have to put them on the agenda unless they violate laws or the bylaws.

As a rule, general meetings could only vote on matters notified in advance, and urgent items other than key matters such as bylaw changes could be voted on separately only with a majority of all members present and at least two-thirds of those present in favor. A cooperative refusing a membership application would have to tell the applicant the reasons and grounds. If the bylaws allow it, the board could decide to hold a general meeting by video conference, with members joining that way counted as present. The cap on total preferred investment would rise from 30% to 50% of equity capital and related amounts.

Business settlement and results reports for a cooperative's founding year would be dropped from management disclosure, and the cooperative survey would move from every two years to every three. The planning and budget minister could request tax information and the number of employment insurance holders from the National Tax Service, the Ministry of Employment and Labor and others every year for the survey. The law would take effect on promulgation, with the information request provisions taking effect six months later.

Citizen Energy View

In citizen solar cooperatives with hundreds of investors, members have little room to be heard if a few board members set the agenda. The right to call meetings and propose items could help put the cooperative principle of one member, one vote into practice. Video general meetings would also be useful for energy cooperatives whose members are spread across several cities and counties. As the preferred investment cap widens, we hope the Assembly's review also considers how to keep investors' and members' interests from diverging.
