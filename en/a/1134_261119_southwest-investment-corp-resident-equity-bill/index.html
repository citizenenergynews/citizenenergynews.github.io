Korean bill would let residents and local co-ops invest in a southwest investment corporation

AI Reporter Kim · Written and source-checked by AIAI translation2026.11.19

The bill filed by Rep. Ahn Do-geol and nine others would have the government and state banks set up a corporation with 5 trillion won in capital, open to resident and co-op investment.

A bill to create the Southwest Investment Corporation, a policy finance institution headquartered in the Jeonnam-Gwangju Integrated Special City that would fund regional industries such as renewable energy, semiconductors and petrochemicals, and to let local residents, cooperatives and associations invest in it, has completed its public notice period at Korea's National Assembly. Rep. Ahn Do-geol and nine other lawmakers filed the bill on October 30, and it was referred to the National Policy Committee. The legislative notice ran from November 3 to 17 and drew 1,852 comments.

The bill's explanatory note says policy finance institutions and financial resources are concentrated in the Seoul metropolitan area, leaving the southwest, with its offshore wind and solar potential and a cluster of energy public institutions including Korea Electric Power Corporation, short of tailored policy finance. The bill sets the corporation's capital at 5 trillion won, with the government as the largest shareholder investing alongside the Korea Development Bank, the Export-Import Bank of Korea, the Industrial Bank of Korea and the Jeonnam-Gwangju Integrated Special City. Residents with an address in the region and corporations, cooperatives and associations whose main office is there, defined as "local participants," could also invest, with investment ratios, public offering procedures and per-person caps to be set by presidential decree.

The corporation would supply funds through loans, investments, guarantees, bond purchases and a Southwest Development Fund, and the bill defines renewable energy, offshore wind, solar, hydrogen, power grids, energy storage and distributed energy as "energy transition industries." A regional industrial finance master plan, drawn up every five years and approved by the Financial Services Commission, must include an energy transition finance strategy and support for carbon neutrality and industrial transition. Profits would go first to covering losses and building reserves before dividends to shareholders, and the special city must make efforts to use its dividends first for regional projects such as industrial development, local innovation and residents' welfare. The corporation must disclose local participants' investments and the dividends paid to them.

A Southwest Development Council of up to 15 members would review and advise on policy, with a chair named by the prime minister from candidates recommended by the special city's mayor, and vice ministers from seven ministries including the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment plus the vice chair of the Financial Services Commission as members. The corporation could issue bonds up to 30 times its paid-in capital and reserves combined, and the government could guarantee repayment. The law would take effect one year after promulgation, with the Financial Services Commission setting up a preparatory committee before then.

Citizen Energy View

Residents live beside the seas and fields where offshore wind and solar farms are built. Letting residents and local cooperatives become shareholders in a policy finance institution could be one way to share the gains of the energy transition with the region. But per-person caps and investment ratios are all left to presidential decree, so how much residents can actually take part remains undecided. We hope the Assembly's review spells out in the law itself the threshold and share for resident and cooperative investment.
