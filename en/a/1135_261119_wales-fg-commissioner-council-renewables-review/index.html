Wales' Future Generations Commissioner opens statutory review of 10 local public bodies' role in renewables, examining public land, community ownership and benefit funds

AI Reporter Kim · Written and source-checked by AIAI translation2026.11.19

Only 3 of the well-being objectives set by 22 councils and 4 corporate joint committees mention energy / Recommendations due after the 2027 local elections

Derek Walker, the Future Generations Commissioner for Wales, on Nov. 18 launched a review of how far local authorities use their land, assets, planning powers, investment decisions and purchasing power to expand renewable energy. The review is carried out under Section 20 of the Well-being of Future Generations Act, the strongest power the commissioner holds. It covers six councils, Conwy, Isle of Anglesey, Newport, Pembrokeshire, Powys and Rhondda Cynon Taf, and the four Corporate Joint Committees for North, Mid, South-East and South-West Wales.

According to the review's terms of reference, only three of the well-being objectives set under the Act by Wales' 22 local authorities and four Corporate Joint Committees refer specifically to energy, and two of those relate narrowly to housing energy efficiency. Wales' renewable capacity stood at around 3.7 GW in 2023, far below Scotland's more than 15 GW of operational capacity reported by Scottish Renewables. The commissioner's office concluded that at the current pace Wales will not meet the Welsh Government's target of generating renewable electricity equal to 100% of consumption by 2035. Walker said, "we're at a standstill – lagging behind other nations."

The review will look at how councils can use their land for renewable generation, support community and local ownership, negotiate for and maximise community benefit funding from developers, and consider new generation in investment decisions such as pensions. The office set six aims, including lower energy bills that free up funding for frontline public services, wealth and income retained in Wales through more community and public ownership, and better protection for farmland and nature. Energy efficiency measures in general, fuel poverty and cutting consumer bills are outside the review's scope.

Evidence gathering will run until March 2027, and findings and recommendations will be published in May or June 2027, after the local government elections. The topic was chosen from six options after two roundtables with 60 contributors and a public survey that drew 51 responses between July and October this year.

Citizen Energy View

The Welsh commissioner's office started from the finding that only three of the well-being objectives of 22 councils deal with energy. How public land, public buildings and car parks are used decides whether the gains from renewable energy flow to outside developers or stay with residents. That is why, in our view, Wales is examining community and public ownership together with benefit funds. Korean local governments, too, should include lists of usable public land and models for resident investment in their energy plans, and check their own progress in carrying them out.
