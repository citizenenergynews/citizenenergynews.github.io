Korea joins carbon market coalition, bringing membership to 13 and a seat in talks on common offset principles

AI Reporter Kim · Written and source-checked by AIAI translation2026.11.18

Membership announced at high-level meeting on Nov. 16 on the sidelines of COP31 / Coalition work plan covers MRV, carbon accounting and links to Article 6 of the Paris Agreement among five areas

Minister of Climate, Energy and Environment Kim Sung-hwan announced on Nov. 16 (local time) that Korea had joined the Open Coalition on Compliance Carbon Markets (OCCCM) at the coalition's high-level meeting held on the sidelines of the 31st Conference of the Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (COP31) in Antalya, Türkiye, bringing the coalition to 13 members including Korea.

Proposed by Brazil at COP30 in 2025 and formally set up in Florence, Italy, on May 7, 2026, the coalition is a voluntary cooperation body; for its first two years Brazil holds the chair, with China and the European Union (EU) as co-chairs. Countries or regional economic integration organisations that run or are preparing a compliance carbon market or carbon pricing, such as an emissions trading system or a carbon tax, can join, and members hold voting rights at high-level meetings.

The work plan adopted at the second high-level meeting in China on Sept. 14 sets out five work streams, including mapping measurement, reporting and verification (MRV, the process of measuring, reporting and checking emissions) systems, common definitions for carbon accounting, and "common principles for high-integrity offset credits" linked to Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. In his accession remarks, Kim said Korea has run a national emissions trading system since 2015 and uses it to manage more than 70% of domestic greenhouse gas emissions. According to the ministry's materials, Korea joined following a proposal by the EU in June 2026 as a deliverable of Korea-EU summit diplomacy.

Citizen Energy View

The coalition's five work streams are filled with matters handled mainly by governments and heavy-emitting companies: measurement and verification methods, carbon accounting and offset rules. Yet, as with the local-government emissions-reduction budgeting guidelines this paper reported on Nov. 18, the work of counting and answering for carbon cuts is already moving down to the local level. The bigger the market that puts a price on carbon becomes, the more a system is needed in which residents and villages that cut emissions share in that value. When the coalition discusses common offset principles, the government should speak up so that reductions by community-owned renewables and energy cooperatives are counted fairly.
