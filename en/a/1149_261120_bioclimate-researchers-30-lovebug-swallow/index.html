Bioclimate researchers map Seoul's lovebug hotspots

AI Reporter Kim · Checked by the editorAI translation2026.11.20

Thirty researchers trained this year by Korea's National Institute of Biological Resources present results on 20 November, drawing on about 24,000 lovebug complaints in Seoul and some 10,000 swallow records.

Thirty "bioclimate research specialists" selected and trained for the first time this year by the National Institute of Biological Resources have produced research on how climate and living things interact. The institute, which belongs to the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment, scheduled its final results presentation for the morning of 20 November at an exhibition hall on its campus in Seohae-gu, Incheon, and released details of four outstanding studies on the 19th. The programme aims to train researchers who can tackle problems where the climate crisis and biodiversity loss overlap; 121 people applied in an open call in March and 30 were selected. Those chosen received monthly pay, research expenses and advice from experts in their fields while carrying out policy research.

A Korea University doctoral student who won the top prize fed about 24,000 complaints about the red-backed lovebug (Plecia longiforceps) lodged with the Seoul city government from 2022 to 2025 into a model to identify areas vulnerable to mass outbreaks. The north-western districts of Eunpyeong, Seodaemun and Mapo and the south-western districts of Yangcheon, Guro and Geumcheon came out as vulnerable, with places where green space meets housing forming the core risk zones. A researcher who has completed doctoral coursework at Ewha Womans University and won an excellence prize analysed about 10,000 swallow sightings from 2000 to 2024 held by the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) alongside weather and land-cover data. Swallows were seen earlier in years with warmer springs, but only 1.8% of grid cells nationwide had data reliable enough to interpret. The researcher therefore proposed a standard record that also logs "not observed" and a citizen-science monitoring system run with schools and local communities.

A Kyonggi University doctoral student used a machine-learning model that includes extreme climate indices such as drought and torrential rain to project future habitats for five Korean amphibian species. Under a high-carbon scenario, habitat suitability for the five species would fall by 36.5% to 52.7% by the 2070s, with mountain species such as the oriental fire-bellied toad expected to decline especially sharply. Hongcheon and Pyeongchang were named as vulnerable areas, and Uiseong and Andong in North Gyeongsang Province as climate refuges. An undergraduate at Hankuk University of Foreign Studies analysed environmental DNA and RNA from surface water in Paldang Lake, laying groundwork for microbial indicators that could give early warning of harmful algal blooms. The institute plans to turn the findings into policy reports and distribute them to bodies under the ministry.

Citizen Energy View

Even with 10,000 swallow records collected, only 1.8% of the country's grid cells had reliable data. A few dozen researchers cannot see on their own how the climate is changing wildlife and villages; the eyes of the people who live there are needed too. Citizen science, in which schools and villages join the monitoring, grows from the same root as residents becoming owners of renewable energy. Rather than leaving the policy reports in a drawer, local governments should turn them into programmes that support resident observer groups and village climate records.
