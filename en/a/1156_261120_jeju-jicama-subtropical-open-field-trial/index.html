Jeju jicama trial grows 1,066g tubers when flowers are removed

AI Reporter Kim · Written and source-checked by AIAI translation2026.11.20

Jeju's eastern agricultural technology centre grew jicama on 700㎡ of open field from May to October. Plots with flowers removed had the highest marketable share, 68.8%, while leaves, stems and seeds are toxic.

An open-field trial of the subtropical crop jicama on Jeju Island produced tubers averaging 1,066.0g each in plots where the flowers were picked off. The Eastern Agricultural Technology Center of the Jeju Special Self-Governing Province Agricultural Research and Extension Services released the results of the May–October trial on 19 November. Jicama is a legume native to Mexico and Central America, grown for its underground tuber, which is eaten raw. The centre ran the trial to find new income crops for farmers as climate change pushes Jeju towards a subtropical climate.

The trial took place on a 700㎡ open-field plot, with seeds sown on 18 May and the crop harvested on 28 October. Planting distances were split into five patterns, from single rows at 40cm to 40×60cm, and in part of the densest 40×20cm plot the flowers were picked twice, on 28 July and 19 August. Without flower removal, tubers in the 40cm plot averaged 908.8g, heavier than those in the 40×20cm plot (690.2g). At the same 40×20cm spacing, the plot with flowers removed produced tubers 54.4% heavier, and its marketable share was 68.8%, 21.1 percentage points higher than without flower removal. In plots where flowers were not removed, the marketable share stayed at 29.3–47.7%.

The weather during the season was uneven. Rainfall in May and June was 105.7–128.7mm higher than the recent five-year figures, while July saw average rainfall of only 1.7mm, bringing a lasting drought. Heavy rain of 277.1mm in October delayed the harvest, stretching the growing period to 164 days against a standard of about 150, and tubers from the flower-removal plot slightly exceeded the usual market standard of 500–1,000g.

Based on the results, the centre will work out suitable planting distances, flower-removal timing and a harvest window of around 150 days, and look for ways to expand sales. Because the leaves, stems and seeds of jicama, everything except the edible tuber, contain the toxic compound rotenone, the centre plans to teach growers how to handle the plant safely.

Citizen Energy View

Spring downpours, a July drought and heavy October rain in a single growing season are field evidence that the climate crisis has already reached Jeju's farmland. Alongside the search for new crops, farm incomes shaken by the weather need another pillar. Income sources that keep coming even in bad weather, such as village-owned solar power, should be designed side by side with farm policy. Local governments should open the way so that climate adaptation trials lead to stable incomes for farms and villages.
