[Busan Sunlight Income ②] Corporate PPAs yield about 2%; researchers propose the city buy the power and save on emission allowances

Reporter Cham · Checked by the editorAI translation2026.11.17

Busan Development Institute: fixed-price cap below 150 won, parking-lot solar sold at 145 won cannot recover its principal / Busan City's allowance purchases rise from 700 million won to over 2 billion won; with the city as PPA buyer, IRR tops 5% / KPX: easing the 300kW high-voltage requirement for direct PPAs is under review / Open discussion: "Leave it to cooperatives"; 1.4km grid gap and departmental silos raised

The researchers who designed the Busan-type Sunlight Income Citizen Participation Project said solar profitability "will fall sharply from next year" and proposed that Busan City step in as a direct power buyer. They spoke at the "2026 Energy New Industry Collaboration Network Forum" held on Nov. 17 at Domoheon, the former official residence of the Busan mayor. Following Part 1 on the project structure, Part 2 sums up the profitability analysis, the explanation of the PPA system and the voices from the floor in the open discussion.

Busan Development Institute: "Sell at 145 won and you can't even recover the principal"

Ki began with the business conditions. "The Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS) ends this year and becomes competitive bidding from next year," he said. "This year's ceiling price in the fixed-price competitive bidding is under 150 won per kWh, and the government is likely to lower it further every year."

His analysis ran as follows. Any public land call is likely to involve parking lots, where solar installation is being made mandatory. Parking-lot solar costs around 2 million won per kW, more than ordinary sites. Under these conditions, selling power at 145 won, near the ceiling, produced a result in which the project "cannot recover the principal."

Selling directly to companies through PPAs offers little relief either. "The market price for corporate PPAs is around 180 won per kWh, but industrial power customers in Busan say industrial electricity rates will fall 10% from next year," Ki said. "It is doubtful whether they would pay that price." Even at the market price of 180 won over 20 years of operation, the internal rate of return (IRR) was only 2.35%.

He also calculated adding the value of greenhouse gas emission allowances to the PPA price. Allowance prices stayed below 10,000 won per ton through Phase 3, then roughly tripled to around 30,000 won after Phase 4 began this year. But a 1MW plant cuts fewer than 600 tons a year, so even reflecting 50% of the carbon value raises the price by only about 7 won per kWh and leaves the IRR at about 3%. Lowering the assumed land rent from 40,000 won to 20,000 won lifts the IRR above 5%. At 10,000 won it is 5–6%, and at zero it reaches the high 6% range. All of these figures use the conservative assumption that the entire cost is paid with equity.

"Busan City as PPA buyer": allowance purchases top 2 billion won

Ki's proposal, which he called "just an idea," is for Busan City to become the PPA buyer itself. Not only companies but also metropolitan governments such as Seoul and Busan take part in the emissions trading scheme. "Since Phase 3, Busan City has had to buy allowances every year, and in 2025 it bought about 70,000 tons," he said. "With allowance prices tripling, a budget that used to be around 700 million won will exceed 2 billion won this year."

The reasoning is that if the city buys power through a PPA, it buys less electricity from KEPCO and also saves on allowance purchases. With both effects fully reflected in the price and land rent set at 10,000 won or zero, the IRR exceeded 5% even with 100% equity. In the more typical structure, where 70% of the cost is borrowed at 5% interest, the return on equity rises further. "There is no buyer as stable as Busan City, so loan rates can also come down," Ki said. But he added, "The various interests within the city are a problem that has to be resolved."

He summed up his presentation in three points. The long-term decline in solar profitability is "not a forecast but a fact," and if most public sites are parking lots, higher upfront costs make things even harder. PPAs have become an essential condition for the project to make money, quite apart from benefiting the region, so contracts should be signed with participants in the emissions trading scheme to build allowance value into the price. And to cut construction costs, find PPA counterparties and lower borrowing costs, operators who know the market and have plenty of relevant experience must take part in the call.

In the Busan citizen survey, more than 60% said they would invest when offered a 3% annual return. Ki noted, however, citing Gyeonggi Province, that "it identified more than 600 public sites and let only cooperatives take part in the calls, but actual use of the identified sites was low."

KPX: "Direct PPAs are for high-voltage customers of 300kW or more; easing the requirement is under review"

Kim Do-hyung, a deputy manager in KPX's VPP business team, compared four ways companies can procure renewable energy: self-generation, renewable PPAs (the direct PPA run by KPX and the third-party PPA run by KEPCO), KEPCO's green premium, and buying renewable energy certificates (RECs). "Self-generation and PPAs procure the electricity physically, while the green premium and RECs are indirect methods where you keep using the same power and pay extra," he explained.

Direct PPAs were made possible by an amendment to the Electricity Business Act in April 2021 and first went into operation in December 2022. Generators and power users sign contracts off-market through a supply intermediary, and the price is set between the parties.

Power users eligible for direct PPAs are high-voltage general or industrial customers of 300kW or more, and most sign contracts of 10 years or longer. Power traded under a PPA does not earn RECs; surplus generation that is not used is sold on the power market, and RECs are issued only for that share.

On-site arrangements, which do not use the KEPCO grid, had their capacity limit removed in July 2025 and pay no grid usage fees. Off-site arrangements, which use the KEPCO grid, must exceed 1MW in combined capacity and pay grid usage fees. Besides hourly settlement, "equal generation settlement," which spreads a month's output evenly across 720 hours, has been in operation since 2026 following a 2025 rule change. Kim said electronic contracts, easing the 300kW high-voltage requirement and a larger role for supply intermediaries in building generation portfolios to balance supply and demand are under review.

As models for local projects, he cited an on-site "local production, local consumption" model, installed on the land of public bodies or local companies and used on the spot, and a model that combines village or small-scale installations above 1MW and joins off-site. Kim also said the REC spot market is set to disappear after a grace period running to 2029 under the RPS overhaul.

Open discussion: "Leave it to the cooperatives"; land, grid lines and rent in focus

In the open discussion that followed, moderated by Choi Kwang-hwan, a professor at Pukyong National University, people from civic groups and cooperatives took the microphone one after another. Choi said Busan could fully meet its renewable energy targets by making good use of idle land and public parking lots. He noted that a government program combining solar with heat pumps will grow from 47 sites this year to more than 300 next year, with Busan among the eligible regions, and suggested widening the effort to cut heat energy as well.

Busan City described the difficulty of securing land. "Public land such as parks and sports facilities is managed by separate property departments, so it doesn't happen just because the energy department proposes a project," a city official said. "Leasing park property requires review and consent procedures, so those departments are reluctant." On the 10MW target, the official said it was chosen to be neither unrealistically large nor too small, and that momentum would build once the first projects are in place.

The chair of a solar cooperative in Busan spoke about a recent study trip to Daegu with members. "In Daegu, six cooperatives signed contracts with the city and have long been running about 2.5MW at 11 sites," he said. "What Busan has failed to do so far comes down to the mayor's will." He asked that small cooperatives also be allowed to join generation projects. Another participant noted that after the Fukushima accident Busan set a 30% renewable target for 2030, which has since been lowered to the 10% range, and asked whether Busan has a one-stop support center like Gyeonggi Province's, handling everything from finding sites and permits to after-care, and whether the techno park plays that role. There was also a call to boldly entrust projects to cooperatives and citizens.

Yoon Won-geun, a division head at Busan Techno Park, replied that the 10MW will be pursued in stages, starting at about 1MW next year, though the details are still under discussion. He said he would propose to the city a task force that gathers citizens' voices from the planning stage, and would discuss with the institute's president expanding the techno park's monitoring center so that one place handles everything from initial installation to operation, monitoring and after-care.

A participant who said he lives in Buk-gu and is a cooperative member pointed out that in the Busan Development Institute survey, more than 35% said they would invest over 10 million won if returns were 6% or more. "In Daegu, site rent was around 5,000 to 10,000 won per kW," he said, arguing that since rent makes a big difference to returns, the survey and profitability analysis should be summarized on a single page for citizens. He also proposed that district offices take turns holding public deliberation forums, like the one Buk-gu held on parking-lot solar.

A participant who has been pursuing a sunlight income village in Gijang-gun described the obstacles on the ground. Because the national program works at the village level, he said, effectively only Gijang-gun among Busan's 16 districts and county could take part, and the site found after much effort is 1.4km from the nearest KEPCO line. KEPCO connects only up to 200m at no cost, so progress has been slow even after setting up a cooperative. He asked about the cost of energy storage systems (ESS) where there are no lines, and about using public land in old downtown areas with many elderly residents. Choi replied that support for grid lines and ESS is included in the government's strategy, and said parking-lot solar construction would cost around 1.8 million won per kW rather than 2 million, while rent of 40,000 won per kW is on the high side.

Ki said "Busan has practically no power plants owned by cooperatives," citing a citizen power cooperative in Ansan, Gyeonggi Province, that does its own construction and links up with other cooperatives. Even at the same 100kW, he added, profitability and construction difficulty differ greatly between a parking lot and a rooftop. He suggested that cooperatives need not handle construction and operation from the start, but could join a special purpose company (SPC) and begin by raising citizen investment and managing dividends. Asked about pricing for small-scale solar after the RPS ends, KPX's Kim said, "I have heard a system favoring installations of 200kW or less is being designed," adding that official details are for the government and the operating agency to announce.

Remaining challenges

Taken together, the presentations and discussion leave Busan's project with three tasks.

The first is land. Busan City said it has been collecting sites since September, but not many have come in because of limited publicity, and its materials note that with no similar precedent, securing land and raising citizen investment may be difficult. The second is buyers. With the fixed-price cap below 150 won per kWh and corporate PPAs at 180 won yielding an IRR of around 2%, a Busan City PPA, reflecting allowance value and cutting land rent were raised. The third is operators: the forum heard that experienced operators who know the market and PPA procedures, along with solid financing costs, are needed.

Busan City plans to build the business model through three or four pilot projects, then keep finding sites and expand the project. The discussion also called for cooperation among the departments that hold land, a council in which civic groups and cooperatives take part from the planning stage, and public forums in each district.

Citizen Energy View

For the Busan project to work as a "citizen dividend model," the citizens' share has to be fixed in numbers first. By the figures presented at the forum, corporate PPAs alone yield an IRR in the 2% range, below even the 3% annual return at which more than 60% of surveyed residents said they would invest. The keys to propping up returns are Busan City acting as a PPA buyer and lower site rents, but both remain at the "idea" and "under review" stage. Citizens can decide whether to invest only if the January 2027 call for proposals spells out whether and at what price the city will buy the power, the site rent, the dividend priority of citizens' preferred shares, and how operating data will be disclosed.
