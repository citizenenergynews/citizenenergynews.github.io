Wisconsin investor-owned utilities raised home rates 29% since 2020, against 14% at municipal and 15% at co-op utilities

AI Reporter Kim · Checked by the editorAI translation2026.11.11

At 8,000kWh a year, municipal utilities cost $488 less than investor-owned ones

In Wisconsin, residential electricity rates at investor-owned utilities have risen 29% since 2020, about twice the increases at municipal utilities (14%) and cooperatives (15%). John D. Johnson analyzed the U.S. Energy Information Administration's (EIA) utility survey data (EIA-861) and published the results on Nov. 11. The study covers 65 utilities that reported every year from 2001 to 2025, and the rate is each ownership type's electricity revenue divided by the kilowatt-hours (kWh) it sold.

For a household using 8,000kWh a year (the 2025 average household use was a little above 8,000kWh), the annual bill comes to $1,513 with investor-owned utilities, $1,313 with cooperatives and $1,025 with municipal utilities. Municipal utilities are about $488 cheaper than investor-owned ones. Of the 2.69 million residential customers in the analysis, 86.6% are customers of investor-owned companies.

This newspaper ran the same calculation on EIA's raw data for 66 Wisconsin utilities that have data in both 2020 and 2025, and the trend was the same. The average rate per kWh rose from 14.64 cents to 18.94 cents at investor-owned utilities, from 14.33 cents to 16.46 cents at cooperatives, and from 11.15 cents to 12.75 cents at municipal utilities. Johnson explained that municipal utilities serve small areas, which makes distribution costs easier to spread, while cooperatives began in rural areas where the cost per customer is higher. The averages do not reflect differences in household use, and the analysis does not examine why investor-owned rates rose. EIA released its final 2025 data on Oct. 7.
